Curriculum objective

Every model is an approximation

By the end, you'll be able to:

  • Speak to a model's results — say what it's actually claiming, in plain terms.
  • Question a number — know what to ask before trusting it.
  • Decide with the output — act on it, eyes open to its limits.

A model is a simplified picture of reality — useful, never the whole truth. The skill is knowing how far to trust it, and where it breaks.

Unit 1 · Precision vs. Recall

The Angler's Dilemma

Catch the fish you want — without hauling in the whole lake.

Prime · The one idea

You can't catch everything and stay clean

Every cast forces the same tension:

Cast wide

You catch almost all the fish you wanted — but your haul is full of junk.

Cast tight

Your haul is clean — but you let a lot of the fish you wanted swim past.

No cast is "correct" — each round, the mistake that costs more decides the cast. The words come after you play.

Debrief · The words for it

Three words for what you just did

You already felt the tradeoff out on the water. Here's what the team calls it:

RecallOf all the fish you wanted, how many you actually caught. Casting wide chases recall.
PrecisionOf everything in your bucket, how much is the fish you wanted — not junk. Casting tight protects precision.
AccuracyHow often you're right overall — but it can look great while hiding a pile of misses.

Debrief · What you felt

What just happened

  • When did you cast wide on purpose — and what made it right?
  • When did you cast tight — and what made a clean catch worth the fish that got away?
  • Which round was hardest to score? Think about its scoring and how the fish were spread.
  • Scooping up everything scores perfectly on recall, terribly on precision. When is that smart — and when is it a disaster?
Debrief · Where it lands

The same dial, at Snapdocs

A different model for every job — but each one sits on the same precision/recall dial, set toward whichever mistake costs more.

▸ Precision vs. recall in the work

  • Routine doc classification: a misfile and a missed file cost about the same → tune for the balanced blend, don't skew either way.
  • Auto-extraction, no human in the loop: a wrong value is expensive to unwind → favor precision; only auto-accept when confident, route the rest to review.
  • Catching a missing signature before a loan funds: a miss is a defective loan → favor recall and tolerate extra human glances.
Unit 2 · Overfitting vs. Underfitting

Weather Forecaster

Fit the days you've already seen — or predict the days you haven't?

Prime · The one idea

Nailing the past isn't the job

Every forecast leans on days you've seen, to call days you haven't. Lean too far either way and it breaks:

Hug every past day

You match the history perfectly — then whiff on the days nobody's seen yet.

Play it too safe

Your read is so stiff it can't bend — and it misses a trend that's really there.

The job is the balance: bend with a real trend, ignore the random wiggles — because you're scored on the days you haven't seen yet.

Debrief · The words for it

Two words for what you just did

You already felt both ways to get it wrong out there. Here's what the team calls it:

OverfittingHugging every past dot until you've memorized the noise. Looks flawless on history, then whiffs on the days nobody's seen. The forecaster that nailed all seven dots.
UnderfittingA read too stiff to bend — missing a real pattern that's genuinely there. The dead-straight line through a real curve.
Debrief · What you felt

What just happened

  • Which forecaster looked best on past days but landed worst on the coming ones?
  • Was there a round where the most complex forecaster was right? What made it right?
  • Was there a round where the simplest forecaster was the right call? What made bending unnecessary?
  • How did you tell "that's a real bend" apart from "that's just noise"?
Debrief · Where it lands

Fitting noise, at Snapdocs

Most of our models read documents — and a document model that memorizes its training files looks brilliant in testing, then falls apart on the next lender's paperwork.

▸ Overfitting vs. underfitting in the work

  • Overfit: a classifier that keys on quirks of the files it trained on — one lender's font, a stray footer, a watermark — aces the test set, then trips on the first unfamiliar template.
  • Underfit: a rule too blunt for the job — "any page that says Disclosure is a Closing Disclosure" — quietly mislabels everything that doesn't fit the cliché.
  • Sometimes the complexity is real: a loan package holds hundreds of document types in endless layouts — reading that honestly needs a model with genuine capacity, not a handful of deterministic rules.
  • The win is matching the model to the true complexity of the documents — not the simplest rule, not the fussiest curve.
Unit 3 · Sample Size & Uncertainty

Fool's Gold

A handful of samples can glitter like gold — but is it the real thing, or just luck?

Prime · The one idea

A small sample can lie — convincingly

Pan a few samples and a creek can glitter like a jackpot — purely by luck. Every call forces the same tension:

Call it early

You save your digging — but a lucky glitter can fool you into a creek that's really empty.

Dig deeper

Each new pan makes the number more trustworthy — but your digging budget runs down fast.

The headline number isn't the story — the range around it is. A few pans give a wide range you can't trust; dig more and it tightens.

Debrief · The words for it

Two words for what you just did

You already felt the pull between calling early and digging more. Here's what the team calls it:

Sample sizeHow many examples a number rests on. "95% accurate" on twenty documents and on twenty thousand read the same on the page — but only one is real evidence. The number of pans you paid for.
Confidence intervalThe range the true number likely falls in — not a single point. A tiny sample gives a wide band; more evidence narrows it. The spread you watched tighten as you dug.
Debrief · What you felt

What just happened

  • Did a creek that looked amazing early turn out to be fool's gold? What tipped you off?
  • Did panning more ever flip a call — a "sure thing" into a bust, or the reverse? What does that say about first impressions?
  • How did the range around a number change your trust versus the number alone?
  • Was there a creek you called with too few samples because pans were scarce? Would more have changed it?
Debrief · Where it lands

Enough evidence, at Snapdocs

A great number on a tiny sample is a hypothesis, not a result.

▸ Sample size & confidence intervals in the work

  • A prompt that nails five test documents isn't proven — five lucky passes say nothing about the thousands of files it hasn't seen.
  • A document type we've only seen a dozen times can't have a trustworthy accuracy yet — rare documents come with a wide interval no matter how good the early numbers look.
  • When a model is "94% accurate," ask what it's measured on — a real result comes with its sample size and a range, not just a headline number.

Summing up

Three games, three ideas — one thread through all of them:
Every model is imperfect; the skill is asking the right question of it.

  • The Angler: which mistake costs more?
  • Weather Forecaster: is this pattern real, or just noise?
  • Fool's Gold: how much evidence is behind this number?

So when someone says "the model says X" — your job is to ask the context question. That instinct is the whole point.
