Curriculum objective

Every model is an approximation

Upon completing this curriculum, you will be able to:

  • Speak to a model's results — follow what the model is actually claiming, and put it in plain terms.
  • Question a number — know what to ask before you trust it.
  • Decide with the output — make the call on what a model tells you, eyes open to its limits.

A model is a simplified picture of reality — genuinely useful, but never the whole truth. It can be wrong in places and still be worth trusting. What matters is knowing how far to trust it, and where it breaks down.

Unit 1 · Precision vs. Recall

The Angler's Dilemma

Catch the fish you want — without hauling in the whole lake.

Prime · The one idea

You can't catch everything and stay clean

Three words that run the whole game:

RecallOf all the fish you wanted, how many you actually caught.
PrecisionOf everything in your bucket, how much is the fish you wanted — not junk.
AccuracyHow often you're right overall — but it can look great while hiding a pile of misses.

Every cast trades these against each other — go wide, or go tight:

Cast wide

You catch almost all the fish you wanted — but your haul is full of junk.

High recall, low precision.

Cast tight

Your haul is clean — but you let a lot of the fish you wanted swim past.

High precision, low recall.

Prime · While you play

The costs decide the cast

There's no single best cast. Each round changes which mistake hurts — and the right one moves with it.

  • Each round tells you which mistake is punished this time. Read that first — it decides everything.
  • When a miss is the costly error, cast wide — even though your catch gets dirtier.
  • When junk is the costly error, cast tight — even though a few good fish escape.
  • Your best possible score is never a perfect haul. It's the right balance for this round's stakes.

Debrief · What you felt

What just happened

  • When did you cast wide on purpose? What about that round made it the right call?
  • When did you cast tight instead? What made keeping a clean catch worth letting good fish swim past?
  • Which round was the hardest to score points on? Given that round's scoring and how the fish were distributed, why was it such a tough problem?
  • Casting wide enough to scoop up everything scores perfectly on recall and terribly on precision. When is that a smart trade — and when is it a disaster?
Debrief · Where it lands

The same dial, at Snapdocs

A different model for every job — but each one sits on the same precision/recall dial, set toward whichever mistake costs more.

▸ Precision vs. recall in the work

  • Routine doc classification: a misfile and a missed file cost about the same → tune for the balanced blend, don't skew either way.
  • Auto-extraction, no human in the loop: a wrong value is expensive to unwind → favor precision; only auto-accept when confident, route the rest to review.
  • Catching a missing signature before a loan funds: a miss is a defective loan → favor recall and tolerate extra human glances.
Unit 2 · Overfitting vs. Underfitting

Weather Forecaster

Fit the days you've already seen — or predict the days you haven't?

Prime · The one idea

Nailing the past isn't the job — neither is ignoring it

Every data science model is built on past data to predict future events. Two words for leaning too far either way:

OverfittingHugging every past dot until you've memorized the noise. Looks flawless on history, then whiffs on the days nobody's seen.
UnderfittingA read too stiff to bend — missing a real pattern that's genuinely there.

The job is the balance: fit the past with just enough flexibility to call the future — bend with a real trend, ignore the random wiggles.

Prime · While you play

Complexity isn't the villain

It's tempting to always pick the simplest forecaster. Don't — sometimes the pattern really does curve.

  • A forecaster that hits all seven past dots exactly is a red flag, not a gold star — ask what it did to the three unseen days.
  • But a dead-straight line can be just as wrong when the real trend genuinely bends.
  • Your eye wants to reward whoever fit the past best. Resist it — you're scoring the future, not the history.

Debrief · What you felt

What just happened

  • Which forecaster looked best on the recorded days but landed worst on the coming ones?
  • Was there a round where the most complex forecaster was actually right? What made it right that time?
  • Was there a round where the simplest forecaster was the right call? What made bending the line unnecessary that time?
  • How did you tell "that's a real bend" apart from "that's just noise"?
Debrief · Where it lands

Fitting noise, at Snapdocs

Most of our models read documents — and a document model that memorizes its training files looks brilliant in testing, then falls apart on the next lender's paperwork.

▸ Overfitting vs. underfitting in the work

  • Overfit: a classifier that keys on quirks of the files it trained on — one lender's font, a stray footer, a watermark — aces the test set, then trips on the first unfamiliar template.
  • Underfit: a rule too blunt for the job — "any page that says Disclosure is a Closing Disclosure" — quietly mislabels everything that doesn't fit the cliché.
  • Sometimes the complexity is real: a loan package holds hundreds of document types in endless layouts — reading that honestly needs a model with genuine capacity, not a handful of deterministic rules.
  • The win is matching the model to the true complexity of the documents — not the simplest rule, not the fussiest curve.
Unit 3 · Sample Size & Uncertainty

Fool's Gold

A handful of samples can glitter like gold — but is it the real thing, or just luck?

Prime · The one idea

A small sample can lie — convincingly

Pan a handful of samples and one creek looks rich — but a tiny sample can glitter by pure luck. Two words decide whether to trust the number:

Sample sizeHow many examples a number rests on. "95% accurate" on twenty documents and on twenty thousand read the same on the page — but only one is real evidence.
Confidence intervalThe range the true number likely falls in — not a single point. A tiny sample gives a wide band; more evidence narrows it.

The headline number isn't the story. How much evidence sits behind it is the story.

Prime · While you play

Read the range, dig where it's in doubt

Every number comes with a confidence interval — a range around it. That range is the real signal.

  • A wide range means "not enough evidence yet" — don't stake a decision on it.
  • A narrow range means the result is holding up. That's when a number earns your trust.
  • Your digging budget is limited. Spend it where the answer is genuinely in doubt, not where it's already obvious.

What just happened

  • Did a creek that looked amazing early turn out to be fool's gold? What tipped you off?
  • Did panning more ever flip a call you were about to make — a "sure thing" into a bust, or the reverse? What does that say about first impressions?

Debrief · Where it lands

Enough evidence, at Snapdocs

A great number on a tiny sample is a hypothesis, not a result.

▸ Sample size & confidence intervals in the work

  • A prompt that nails five test documents isn't proven — five lucky passes say nothing about the thousands of files it hasn't seen.
  • A document type we've only seen a dozen times can't have a trustworthy accuracy yet — rare documents come with a wide interval no matter how good the early numbers look.
  • When a model is "94% accurate," ask what it's measured on — a real result comes with its sample size and a range, not just a headline number.

Summing up

Three games, three ideas — and one thread running through all of them:
Every model is imperfect, and the skill that matters is asking the right question of it.

  • The Angler: which mistake costs more?
  • Weather Forecaster: is this pattern real, or just noise?
  • Fool's Gold: how much evidence is behind this number?

So when someone says "the model says X" — your job is to ask the context question. That instinct is the whole point.
